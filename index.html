China — Republic (KMT)

Nationalist China under Chiang Kai-shek
The war broke the Nationalist state militarily, economically, and morally — emptying the political space that the Communists would soon fill.
Human Cost
15–20 million dead. ~95 million displaced — the largest refugee crisis of the 20th century. Nanking (1937): ~200,000–300,000 killed.
Social
Eight years of total war militarised rural society and discredited urban elites who had fled inland. Trauma from atrocities (Unit 731, comfort women) hardened anti-Japanese sentiment that endures today.
Political
The GMD emerged exhausted while the CCP grew from ~40,000 (1937) to ~1.2 million members (1945). Source L: at Japan's surrender the CCP defied Chiang's stand-down orders, seizing the strategic initiative.
Economic
Hyperinflation under the GMD destroyed middle-class savings (1945–49 prices rose ~2,500%). Infrastructure devastation delayed industrial recovery until the PRC's First Five-Year Plan (1953).
Verdict: The Pacific War did not cause the Communist victory — but it made it possible. By 1949, the Republic of China had been reduced to Taiwan.

Chinese Communist Base Areas

Yan'an and the guerrilla zones, 1937–45
For the CCP, the war was a forge — converting a hunted insurgency into a mass movement and a state-in-waiting.
Strategic Growth
Membership rose from ~40,000 in 1937 to ~1.2 million by 1945; "liberated zones" expanded to encompass ~100 million people behind Japanese lines.
Political Legitimacy
By resisting Japan in the north (while the GMD fell back to Chongqing), the CCP earned a reputation as defenders of the nation — vital nationalist credibility for the post-war contest.
Source L — The Surrender Moment
Fenby: "Two days before Hirohito surrendered, the Communists rejected an instruction from [Chiang] for their forces not to advance... The new invaders [Soviets] handed them 100,000 guns from Japanese stores, and 200,000 Manchukuo troops were recruited."
Verdict: The CCP entered the post-war civil war armed, mobilised, and morally legitimised — outcomes inconceivable without the Japanese invasion.

Empire of Japan

From aggressor empire to demilitarised democracy
Defeat brought reckoning, reinvention, and — through the Korean War — recovery. Japan emerged stripped of empire, but reshaped into a peaceful and ultimately prosperous state.

Social — War Crimes & Reckoning
Tokyo Tribunal (1946–48) tried 28 Class-A war criminals. Unit 731 biological-weapons program, the "comfort women" system, the Nanking massacre, and POW abuses (Bataan, Sandakan) exposed. Hirohito spared by U.S. for occupation stability — a decision that left wounds across Asia unhealed.
Political — New Constitution
1947 Constitution renounced war (Article 9), enfranchised women, dissolved the zaibatsu, and reformed land tenure. Implemented under SCAP / General MacArthur.
Economic — Source K
Gordon: the Korean War was "a gift of the gods." U.S. procurements 1951–53 ≈ US\$2 billion — roughly 60% of Japan's exports. Exports tripled 1949–51; production rose ~70%; GNP grew at double-digit rates. The occupation ended in 1952 — far sooner than initially planned.
Verdict: No major power in history was so totally remade by defeat. Yet Japan's recovery rested partly on Korean tragedy — a moral irony that Gordon notes was "tastelessly celebrated" by its leaders.

Manchukuo

The Japanese puppet state, 1932–1945
Manchukuo was Imperial Japan's industrial laboratory — a colony of factories, forced labour, and atrocity, whose post-war assets fell decisively to the Communist side.
The Colonial Experiment
Nominally ruled by the last Qing emperor Puyi; in reality administered by the Kwantung Army. Heavy industry concentrated here (steel, coal, chemicals) supplied 30% of Japan's war economy by 1944.
Atrocity
Unit 731 (Harbin): biological and chemical experiments on Chinese, Korean, and Soviet prisoners. Estimated 200,000+ victims of forced labour and medical experimentation.
August 1945 — Source L
Soviet invasion (Operation August Storm, 9 Aug 1945) crushed the Kwantung Army in days. As Fenby notes, the CCP "brought back units that had been sheltering in Siberia" and recruited 200,000 ex-Manchukuo troops — a critical injection for the civil war.
Verdict: Manchukuo's industrial inheritance and military stockpiles flowed to the CCP after 1945 — a direct material legacy linking Japanese colonialism to Communist victory.

Korea

Liberation, partition, and the war's deepest open wound
Liberation from 35 years of Japanese rule (1910–1945) was instantly followed by partition — a tragedy that ironically rescued Japan's economy (Source K).
Social — Under Japanese Rule
Forced labour: ~500,000 Koreans conscripted to Japanese mines and factories. ~200,000 women coerced into the "comfort women" system. Cultural suppression: Korean language banned in schools (1938), Japanese names imposed (1939).
Political — Division
August 1945: U.S. and USSR divided the peninsula at the 38th parallel as a temporary measure. By 1948 two rival regimes were proclaimed: DPRK (Kim Il-sung) and ROK (Syngman Rhee). Korean War 1950–53: ~3 million dead, division entrenched.
Economic
Industrial assets concentrated in the North (hydroelectric, chemical); the South was agrarian. Partition severed an integrated economy, condemning both halves to decades of dislocation — but, per Source K, "conferred great fortune" on Japan.
Verdict: Korea bore the costs of both Japanese empire and superpower confrontation — its partition remains the most enduring unresolved legacy of the Second World War in Asia.

French Indochina

Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia — and the death of European empire
Japan's 1945 destruction of French rule made the restoration of European colonialism impossible. Indochina became the symbolic graveyard of empire in Asia.
Human Cost
The Vietnam famine of 1944–45 — caused by Japanese rice requisitioning and French quotas — killed an estimated 1–2 million people in Tonkin alone.
Political Vacuum
9 March 1945: Japan launched a coup against the Vichy French administration, shattering 80 years of European prestige. Hồ Chí Minh's Việt Minh filled the vacuum, declaring independence on 2 September 1945.
The Wars of Decolonisation
France attempted reconquest: First Indochina War (1946–54), ended catastrophically at Điện Biên Phủ. Per Source N, Vietnam achieved independence in 1945 but fought France until 1954; Laos (1953), Cambodia (1953).
Source M — The Wider Pattern
Thompson: across East Asia "the old order had receded and a new order had taken its place." Lee Kuan Yew's generation emerged "determined that no one — neither the Japanese nor the British — had the right to push and kick us around."
Verdict: Indochina is the case study par excellence — the war did not merely weaken European empire, it killed it. Source N's map shows the same pattern repeated across the region.

Self-Determination — The End of Empires

A region-wide reckoning, 1945–1997
The war's deepest ideological legacy was the death of the colonial idea in Asia. Source N records 16 instances of decolonisation across the Pacific theatre between 1946 and 2002.
Cascade of Independence (Source N)
1945 — Indonesia (recognised 1949); Vietnam (DRV) declared.
1946 — Philippines (from USA).
1948 — Burma (from Britain).
1949–53 — Laos (from France, staged).
1953 — Cambodia (from France).
1957–63 — Malaysia and Singapore (from Britain).
1975 — Papua New Guinea (from Australia).
1986 — Federated States of Micronesia (from USA).
1997 — Hong Kong returned to China.
Why the Empires Fell
Military: Japan's 1941–42 conquests destroyed the myth of white invincibility — Singapore (1942) was Britain's worst-ever capitulation.
Economic: European powers emerged bankrupt and unable to finance reconquest.
Ideological: The Atlantic Charter (1941) committed Allies to self-determination — a principle Asian nationalists invoked.
Indigenous: Wartime occupation politicised entire generations (Source M — Lee Kuan Yew).
Verdict: The Pacific War was the largest single decolonising event in modern history. Sources M and N together demonstrate that no European empire survived its impact intact.

Source Analysis & Evaluation

Four historians on the war's legacy — Sources K, L, M, N
SOURCE K

Andrew Gordon — A Modern History of Japan (OUP, 2003)

Academic monograph by a Harvard historian of modern Japan.

CONTENT: Argues the Korean War transformed Japan economically: ~US\$2 billion in U.S. procurements (≈60% of exports, 1951–53); exports tripled 1949–51; GNP grew at double-digit rates; occupation ended after only seven years.

ANALYSIS: Gordon frames the Korean War as the catalyst — not the foundation — of Japanese recovery, which depended on earlier SCAP reforms. His wry language ("tastelessly celebrated," "gift of the gods") signals moral discomfort at Japan profiting from Korean suffering.

EVALUATION: Highly reliable: peer-reviewed, scholarly, balanced. A limitation is its Japan-centric lens — the human cost across the strait is acknowledged only obliquely.

SOURCE L

Jonathan Fenby — Modern China: The Fall and Rise of a Great Power (HarperCollins, 2008)

Popular history by a former Reuters and South China Morning Post editor.

CONTENT: Records that two days before Japan's surrender, the CCP defied Chiang's stand-down order, advanced into Japanese-held territory, linked with Soviet forces, seized 100,000 Japanese weapons, and recruited 200,000 former Manchukuo troops.

ANALYSIS: Frames August 1945 as a decisive turning point of the Chinese Civil War — direct material evidence that the Pacific War's end enabled the 1949 Communist victory.

EVALUATION: Reliable and accessible; relies on synthesised secondary sources rather than archival research. The brevity risks oversimplifying a complex civil-war transition.

SOURCE M

Robert Smith Thompson — Empires on the Pacific (Basic Books, 2001)

Diplomatic history; U.S. academic; broad Pacific-wide focus.

CONTENT: Describes the symbolic 1945 restoration of European authority (Union Jack over Hong Kong; Mountbatten at Singapore) — but notes the local Chinese population "unwilling again to accept colonialism." Quotes Lee Kuan Yew (1961): his generation emerged "determined that no one — neither the Japanese nor the British — had the right to push and kick us around."

ANALYSIS: Identifies a psychological turning point — the war shattered colonial mystique. The Lee Kuan Yew quotation lends authoritative indigenous voice, linking wartime experience to post-war nationalism.

EVALUATION: Strong narrative reliability; however, use of a 1961 retrospective quotation risks projecting later political identity onto 1945 attitudes — a mild presentist bias.

SOURCE N

Map — Decolonisation in South-Eastern Asia and the Pacific

Reference cartographic source listing 16 decolonisations, 1946–2002.

CONTENT: Plots chronology and colonial origin of each new state, from the Philippines (1946, USA) to East Timor (2002, Indonesia).

ANALYSIS: Visually corroborates the textual sources: a sweeping continent-wide collapse of European and U.S. imperial control following WWII, clustered most densely in 1945–1957.

EVALUATION: Useful for scope and scale, but limited alone — it shows what happened, not why. Must be read alongside Sources K–M for causation.

SYNTHESIS

Convergent Testimony

Read together, the four sources construct a coherent account of the war's legacy: K shows Japan's recovery (economic), L shows China's revolution (political), M shows the death of the colonial idea (ideological), and N maps the result (cartographic). Each on its own is partial; their convergence is historiographically powerful.

LIMITATION: All four are Western-authored. The voices of Asian peasants, soldiers, "comfort women," and ordinary civilians — those who paid the greatest cost — are mediated through these analytical lenses rather than heard directly.

LEST WE FORGET
In memory of the millions of Asian and Pacific peoples — soldiers, civilians, the conscripted and the colonised — whose lives were taken or transformed by the Second World War, 1937–1945.
